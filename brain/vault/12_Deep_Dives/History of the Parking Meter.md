# History of the Parking Meter

The first machine to price the kerb. The world's first parking meter — Carl C. Magee's "Park-O-Meter No. 1" — was installed in **Oklahoma City on 16 July 1935**, invented by a newspaperman on the chamber of commerce's traffic committee to break the all-day parkers' occupation of shopping streets. Merchants who predicted ruin discovered turnover: priced kerb, more customers. Within a decade meters colonised America; the *Autogerechte* decades exported them worldwide ([[Autogerechte Stadt]]).

The meter matters theoretically as the **first admission that kerb space is scarce and valuable** — the crack in "free parking" seventy years before Shoup widened it into a discipline ([[Donald Shoup]], [[Parking Free for 99 Percent of Trips]]). Shoup's performance pricing — the rate that keeps one space open per block — is the meter's idea completed ([[Parking Reform]]).

For the project the meter is a transitional fossil: the Hub City ends kerb storage rather than pricing it, but the meter's lesson — *space behaves when it is valued* — powers the funding logic of the transition years ([[Nottingham Workplace Parking Levy]], [[Funding Stack]]).
