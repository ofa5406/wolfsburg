# Braess Paradox

The mathematics that makes street removal respectable. Dietrich Braess (1968, Ruhr-Universität Bochum) proved that **adding a link to a network in which users route selfishly can make *everyone's* journey slower** — and, symmetrically, that deleting a link can speed the whole network up. The mechanism: individually rational shortcuts concentrate flow onto shared bottlenecks; the equilibrium of selfish routing is not the social optimum.

Reality has obliged with demonstrations: Seoul's Cheonggyecheon — an elevated motorway demolished for a stream park, traffic *improved*; New York's 42nd Street closures; Stuttgart's own added-road slowdown in the original literature ([[Traffic Evaporation]]).

For the project, Braess upgrades the zone structure's defence from empirical to structural: filtering cross-links out of the centre is not a sacrifice traded for liveability — **it can be a network improvement in pure travel-time terms**, before a single tree is planted ([[Filtered Permeability]], [[Five-Zone Model]], [[Fundamental Law of Road Congestion]]). The counter-intuitive is, here, the well-proven.
