# The Death and Life of Great American Cities

The book that opened the counter-tradition (1961). Jane Jacobs' attack on orthodox planning begins with the sentence "This book is an attack on current city planning and rebuilding" and delivers exactly that: against the towers-in-parks and urban motorways of high modernism, she set the observed micro-mechanics of working streets — the sidewalk ballet, eyes on the street, the four generators of diversity (mixed primary uses, short blocks, aged buildings, concentration) ([[Jane Jacobs]], [[Eyes on the Street]]).

Its method was its scandal: no models, no surveys of the orthodox kind — just disciplined observation of what streets actually do, from a woman the establishment dismissed. Sixty years of subsequent research largely vindicated her mechanisms, and the book became the discipline's most cited text.

The project reads it as the origin of its own genre: the claim that **street life is infrastructure** — measurable, designable, destroyable by car dominance — which the Gehl tradition turned into method ([[Jan Gehl]]) and this project turns into a computable score ([[Social Vitality Scoring]], [[Public Life]]).
