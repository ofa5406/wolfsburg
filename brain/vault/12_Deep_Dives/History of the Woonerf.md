# History of the Woonerf

The street that domesticated the car, born in Delft. In the late 1960s, Delft residents and the urbanist **Niek de Boer** (with the municipality's engineers) began rebuilding residential streets so the carriageway disappeared: one continuous paved surface, curves and planting forcing walking pace, doorsteps and play claiming the middle. De Boer named it the **woonerf** — "living yard": the street redefined as the shared ground of the homes around it ([[Woonerf]]).

The Netherlands gave it legal teeth in **1976**, writing the *woonerf* into the traffic code: pedestrians may use the full width, play is permitted, drivers are guests at walking pace. Germany's *Spielstraße*, Britain's home zones and the generalised 2000s "shared space" all descend from it ([[Hans Monderman]]).

In the project's lineage the woonerf is the **first built proof that street design outperforms regulation** — behaviour reshaped by geometry, not signage ([[Traffic Calming]], [[Human Scale]]). The hub's Zone 3 dwelling edge is a woonerf condition by another name: the surface continuous, the vehicle subordinate, the front door sovereign ([[Zone 3 — Dwelling Edge]], [[Dutch Mobility Turn]]).
