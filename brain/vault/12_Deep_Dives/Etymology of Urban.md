# Etymology of Urban

**Urban** comes from Latin *urbs* — the walled city, Rome above all — via *urbanus*, "of the city," which from the start carried a second meaning: refined, witty, courteous. The doublet survives intact in English: *urban* (of the city) and *urbane* (polished) are the same word twice, and their shared descent preserves an ancient claim — **city air makes manners**; the crowd teaches civility.

Antiquity's counter-word was *rusticus*; modernity's is *suburbanus* — Rome's own term for the villa belt *below the city* — making sprawl a two-thousand-year-old vocabulary item.

The 19th century industrialised the word: Cerdà had to invent *urbanización* in 1867 because no term existed for city-making as deliberate practice ([[Ildefons Cerdà]]) — urbanism is younger than the railway. The project's own coinage sits knowingly in that tradition: [[Urban Proof]] asks the *urbanus* claim to be demonstrated, not assumed — that the post-car street will in fact produce the civility its lineage promises ([[Public Life]], [[Walkability]]).
