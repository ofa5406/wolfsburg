# Etymology of Traffic

**Traffic** was trade before it was congestion. The word arrives in English around 1500 from French *trafique* and Italian *traffico* (from *trafficare*, to trade — earlier origins disputed, possibly via a Vulgar Latin *transfricare*, "to rub across"). For four centuries traffic meant **commerce** — the traffic in silk, in ideas; a trafficker was a merchant.

The migration to vehicles came stepwise: 18th-century usage covered the *movement of goods*, 19th-century railways spoke of passenger traffic, and by the motor age the word had completed its inversion — from the exchange itself to the vehicles carrying it, and finally to their mutual obstruction. "Traffic" now names the *failure* of what it once named the success of.

The etymology carries a design insight the project uses: streets exist for *exchange*, not for vehicle movement per se ([[Street as Public Space]], [[Agora]]) — and when the vehicles crowd out the exchange, the word's older meaning indicts its newer one ([[Traffic Evaporation]], [[Car Dependency]]). Reclaiming the street returns traffic to its first sense: commerce, encounter, the trade of daily life ([[Public Life]]).
