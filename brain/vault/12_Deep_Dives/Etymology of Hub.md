# Etymology of Hub

The project's central word began as a piece of a wheel. **Hub** enters English in the 16th century (probably a variant of *hob*, the projection at a fireplace) as the name for the **solid centre of a wheel — the block the spokes radiate from and the axle turns in**. For three centuries it meant exactly that: the load-bearing middle of a rotating system.

The metaphor arrived in the 19th century — famously in the American quip that Boston was "the hub of the universe" — and the 20th century made it technical: aviation's hub-and-spoke networks, then telecoms and computing, where a hub is **the node through which a network's connections concentrate** ([[Mobility Hub]]).

The etymology quietly argues the thesis. A hub is not a *terminus* (where movement stops, like parking) but a *centre of rotation* — the still point that makes the whole wheel work. `<stadt.hub>` reads literally: the city given a new load-bearing centre, spokes of walking, cycling and pooled vehicles radiating from it ([[Hub City]], [[Hub as Infrastructure]], [[Etymology of Stadt]]).
