# Urban Heat Island

The thermal case against asphalt. The **urban heat island** effect — cities running several degrees warmer than their surroundings — was first documented by Luke Howard's London observations in 1818 and is now a mortality statistic: dark, sealed surfaces absorb solar radiation by day and release it through the night, denying the cooling pause that heat-stressed bodies need. Barcelona's superblock health model attributes **117 prevented deaths a year to heat reduction alone** ([[Barcelona Health Dividend]]).

Parking is the effect's ideal accomplice: low-albedo asphalt sheets, no shade, no evapotranspiration — a car park is a heat battery the size of a block ([[Car-Dedicated Land]], [[Sponge City]]).

The remedy is the reclamation programme read thermally: every converted lot trades absorption for shade and evapotranspiration — trees, planting, permeable ground ([[Tree with Stone Grate]], [[Planting Bed and Bioswale]], [[Reclaimed Land]]). As climate adaptation, the post-car city is also simply the *cooler* city — a benefit that polls across every political line the mobility argument divides ([[Public-Space Dividend]]).
