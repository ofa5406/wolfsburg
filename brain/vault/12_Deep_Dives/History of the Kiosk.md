# History of the Kiosk

The biography of the smallest public building. The kiosk's arc runs: **Ottoman garden pavilion** (the köşk — open-sided, roofed, a place to sit above the garden; [[Etymology of Kiosk]]) → **European garden folly** (18th-century "Turkish kiosks" in French and German parks) → **street infrastructure** (Paris licenses newspaper kiosks on the grands boulevards from 1857; Berlin follows with its *Litfaßsäule* cousins and drinking kiosks) → **utility capsule** (telephone kiosks, ticket booths, the German *Büdchen* and *Trinkhalle* corner-kiosk culture) → **interface** (the self-service screen that borrowed the name).

Through every mutation the type keeps three properties: **small footprint, public orientation, and constant occupancy** — someone is always there, which is why kiosks anchor informal street life far beyond their size: the vendor is Jacobs' eyes on the street employed full-time ([[Eyes on the Street]], [[Jane Jacobs]]).

That is precisely the role the hub palette assigns it: the [[Vendor Kiosk Slot]] and [[Gastronomy and Permanent Programme]] give each hub its resident witness — the smallest building carrying the largest social load ([[Hub as Place]], [[Catalyst Not Shelter]]).
