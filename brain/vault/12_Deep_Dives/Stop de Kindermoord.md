# Stop de Kindermoord

The protest that turned a country. In 1971, Dutch roads killed over 3,000 people, more than 400 of them children. The journalist **Vic Langenhoff**, after losing his own child, wrote the article whose headline became a movement: ***Stop de Kindermoord*** — "Stop the Child Murder." Parents blocked streets, occupied accident sites, staged bicycle demonstrations; a future prime minister chaired the group.

Together with the 1973 oil crisis — which gave the Netherlands its car-free Sundays, a nation experiencing empty motorways as a revelation — the movement forced the pivot that made Dutch cycling infrastructure national policy ([[Dutch Mobility Turn]], [[History of the Woonerf]]).

Its lesson is the one the project's consent strategy is built on: **car-reduction politics wins when it is framed as protection, not restriction** — nobody marched against cars; they marched for children ([[Imposition Without Consent]]). The equivalent Wolfsburg frame is ready-made: safe routes for Lukas, a Tuesday market for Gertrude — the system argued through its most defensible beneficiaries ([[Lukas]], [[Equity of the Post-Car Transition]]).
