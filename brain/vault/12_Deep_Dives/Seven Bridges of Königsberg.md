# Seven Bridges of Königsberg

Where network thinking began — with a walking puzzle. Königsberg's citizens asked whether one stroll could cross each of the city's seven bridges exactly once. **Leonhard Euler proved in 1736 that it could not** — and in proving it, invented the abstraction the project's tools still run on: throw away distance and geometry, keep only *nodes and connections*, and the question becomes computable. Graph theory is urban analysis's oldest export.

The direct line runs from Euler's landmasses-and-bridges to the toolchain: the road network as weighted graph, Dijkstra finding least-cost paths through it ([[Rad Network Algorithm]]), connectivity counted per district ([[Mobility Mode]]), the hub network designed as nodes-and-links coverage ([[Multimodal Network]], [[Street Connectivity]]).

And to this artifact itself: the brain is a graph because the founding insight holds for knowledge as for bridges — **structure lives in the connections** ([[Project Brain]], [[Zettelkasten]]). That a city's walking puzzle started it is the kind of symmetry the exhibition can enjoy: urbanism gave mathematics the graph; the graph returns to draw the city.
