# Radburn Layout

Filtered permeability's American grandparent — and its cautionary tale. **Radburn, New Jersey** (1929, Clarence Stein and Henry Wright), the self-described "town for the motor age," pioneered the complete separation of people and cars: superblocks with houses facing shared green interiors, a pedestrian path network crossing roads by underpass, cul-de-sac service access — a child could walk to school without meeting a vehicle.

Its DNA spread everywhere: British new towns, Scandinavian suburbs, and — through the same mid-century channels — German *autogerecht* planning absorbed the separation principle ([[Autogerechte Stadt]]).

The double lesson is why the note exists. Radburn proved **network asymmetry works**: give walking its own connected grid and protection follows — the ancestor of Houten's diagram and the woonerf's street ([[Houten]], [[Filtered Permeability]]). But its *segregation* version — pedestrians exiled to the back, streets surrendered to cars — hollowed the street as public space ([[Street as Public Space]]). The project takes the asymmetry and refuses the exile: filter the car, keep everyone on the same living surface ([[Woonerf]], [[Modal Filter]]).
