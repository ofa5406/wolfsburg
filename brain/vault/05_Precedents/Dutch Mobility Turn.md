# Dutch Mobility Turn

The national-scale existence proof. In the early 1970s the Netherlands — as car-committed as any European country, demolishing neighbourhoods for urban motorways — reversed course. Triggered by the *Stop de Kindermoord* road-safety movement and the 1973 oil crisis, Dutch planning shifted from car priority to pedestrians, cyclists and public transport, and then compounded that choice for fifty years: continuous cycle networks, woonerven ([[Woonerf]]), filtered city centres ([[Groningen Traffic Circulation Plan]]), and new towns built around the bicycle ([[Houten]]).

The result is the baseline the whole field measures against: the post-car city is not a rendering — it exists at national scale, wealthy, functioning and ordinary. Dutch mobility is what "mature" looks like for every argument the project makes about modal shift, street design and access-over-ownership ([[Post-Car City]]).

The transfer lesson is temporal honesty: the Netherlands took two generations. Wolfsburg's plan compresses the trajectory by starting from the Dutch endpoint's toolbox — proven zone logic, proven street sections — rather than rediscovering it, with the hub network and autonomy as the acceleration the 1970s lacked ([[Implementation Timeline]]).
