# Stockholm Congestion Charge

The consent-engineering precedent: **trial first, vote after**. Stockholm introduced its congestion charge as a temporary trial; traffic fell ~22% against a 10–15% target, air quality and travel-time reliability improved — and public acceptance, hostile before the trial, **rose once people experienced the benefits**. A referendum then locked the scheme in permanently, and the reductions persisted over the following years (Eliasson's five-year evaluation).

London's parallel case corroborates the traffic effect — a 20–30% drop in the charged zone within a year — while showing that reallocating the freed road space (as London did to buses and bikes) determines whether congestion relief endures.

The transferable finding is about the *sequence of experience and decision*: people vote differently about a change they have lived than one they have imagined. That is the entire architecture of the project's phasing — reversible pilots, visible benefits, then permanence — applied by a capital city to its most contested policy ([[Tactical Urbanism]], [[Imposition Without Consent]]). Wolfsburg's zone entrances follow the same path: trial season first, referendum-grade consent after ([[Five-Zone Model]]).
